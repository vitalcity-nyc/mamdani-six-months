Mamdani's first six months: the promise ledger

Zohran Mamdani took office as mayor of New York City on Jan. 1, 2026. Six months in, here is where each of his signature campaign promises stands — what has been delivered, what is moving, what Albany refused and where there has been no visible action — plus what he has done that he never promised.

The promises Fifteen signature commitments from the 2025 campaign, ordered by how far each has gotten.
Housing

Freeze the rent

The promise: freeze rents for roughly 1 million rent-stabilized apartments through appointments to the Rent Guidelines Board.

Delivered

On June 25 the board — with six of its nine members appointed by Mamdani — voted 7–1 to freeze rents on both one- and two-year leases beginning between Oct. 1, 2026 and Sept. 30, 2027, the first two-year freeze in the board's history. One member resigned in protest the day of the vote. Gothamist ↗

Needed Mayoral appointments alone — the one big promise fully within City Hall's power.

Public safety

Keep Jessica Tisch atop the NYPD

The promise: retain Jessica Tisch as commissioner of the New York Police Department.

Delivered

Announced Nov. 19, 2025, before inauguration; Tisch has served as police commissioner through the full six months, with public friction over the canceled plan to hire more officers and over shifting mental-health calls away from police. THE CITY ↗

Needed A mayoral appointment — and Tisch's agreement to stay.

Childcare

Universal childcare

The promise: free childcare for every New York City child aged 6 weeks to 5 years.

In progress

One week into the term, Gov. Kathy Hochul and Mamdani jointly announced a state-funded path, including free care for 2-year-olds in New York City ("2-Care") starting with about 2,000 seats in high-need neighborhoods in fall 2026, backed by $73 million in first-year state funding within a $1.7 billion statewide expansion. Full universality remains years away, and the state has committed to fund only the program's first two years. Governor's office ↗ · New York Focus ↗

Needed Albany funding — and it materialized: the standout case of state cooperation.

Cost of living

City-owned grocery stores

The promise: a pilot of five city-owned grocery stores, one per borough, priced at roughly $60 million.

In progress

Two of five sites announced: La Marqueta in East Harlem (April) and The Peninsula in Hunts Point (May). None has opened — the first store is projected for 2027 and La Marqueta by 2029. The city has allocated roughly $70 million in capital funds for the five-store program, up from the $60 million campaign estimate, with the first two stores accounting for a reported $40 million. Sites in Brooklyn, Queens and Staten Island are still being scouted. Mayor's office ↗ · Bronx Times ↗

Needed City capital dollars and Economic Development Corporation machinery — both moving.

Labor

$30 minimum wage by 2030

The promise: raise New York City's minimum wage to $30 an hour by 2030.

In progress

A City Council bill introduced in March would phase large employers to $30 an hour by 2030 and smaller ones to $29 by 2031, with inflation increases after. It remains in committee — and the state, which controls the wage floor, has taken no public step toward giving the city that power. Gothamist ↗ · Int 0757-2026 ↗

Needed City Council passage plus, almost certainly, Albany authorization. Neither has happened yet.

Immigration

Shield immigrant New Yorkers

The promise: "Trump-proof" the city, including $165 million for immigrant legal services.

In progress

A Feb. 6 executive order bars U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement from city-run facilities without a judicial warrant and restricts data-sharing with federal immigration authorities; the federal Department of Homeland Security publicly objected. The budget adopted June 30 restores $86.4 million for immigration legal services; no public accounting shows the full $165 million pledge funded. CBS New York ↗ · City Council ↗

Needed Executive action (taken) plus city budget dollars (partly delivered) — against active federal resistance.

Housing

200,000 affordable homes

The promise: build 200,000 publicly subsidized, permanently affordable homes over 10 years, originally financed by roughly $70 billion in new city borrowing.

In progress

The "Block by Block" plan released May 26 keeps the 200,000-new-homes target, adds 200,000 preserved and sets development and preservation goals more ambitious than the Bloomberg or de Blasio housing plans. The financing method changed: with Gov. Kathy Hochul signaling opposition to major new borrowing and Albany providing no new capacity, the plan pairs $22 billion in city capital over five years with state, federal and private financing. A day-one executive order also created the SPEED task force, whose May reform package aims to cut about eight months from affordable-project timelines and up to two years from projects needing rezonings. Hell Gate ↗ · The Real Deal ↗ · amNewYork ↗

Needed City Council capital budgets (first tranche secured) and, over time, state and federal subsidy; the promised homes themselves are a decade-long test.

Governance

A government that delivers

The promise: make city government itself work — "no problem too large for government to solve, and no concern too small for it to care about," with excellence "the expectation across government, not the exception." Victory speech ↗

In progress

Delivery has been treated as its own agenda: a day-one executive order created the SPEED housing-delivery task force, a late-January order required a chief savings officer in every agency and a new Office of Mass Engagement consolidated the city's scattered outreach units. The pothole blitzes became his stated proof of concept — 100,000 filled by April 6, per the transportation department the most in the first 100 days of a year in over a decade — "symptomatic of a city government that can actually take care of even the smallest tasks." On May 28 he launched the Commission on Government Efficiency, a charter revision commission chaired by Patrick Gaspard now holding hearings toward November 2026 ballot proposals. ABC7 ↗ · CBS New York ↗ · Al Jazeera ↗

Needed Mayoral orders and appointments so far; the efficiency commission's proposals go to voters in November.

Public safety

Department of Community Safety

The promise: a new roughly $1-billion-a-year department to take mental-health crisis calls off the Police Department's plate.

Scaled back

What launched March 19 by executive order is a mayoral Office of Community Safety — not a department — budgeted around $260–270 million, largely reallocated from existing programs and centered on expanding the B-HEARD mental-health crisis-response teams. Commissioner Tisch has publicly estimated only about 2% of calls for service would shift away from police. PBS NewsHour ↗ · Crain's ↗

Needed City Council funding and likely legislation for a full department — neither at promised scale.

Housing

Expand CityFHEPS rental vouchers

The promise: drop the city's appeal in the lawsuit over expanding CityFHEPS rental vouchers on day one and grow the program that moves homeless New Yorkers into housing.

Scaled back

The administration did not drop the appeal on day one and in February stepped back from expansion, citing the program's cost growth from an initial $25 million to $1.1 billion by fiscal 2025 — advocates said they felt "betrayed." In June it settled the lawsuit, and the budget deal funded a new voucher expansion: $175 million next year (and $125 million the year after), reaching up to 30,000 more New Yorkers. amNewYork ↗ · Gothamist ↗

Needed City budget dollars — delivered in part, after a public reversal.

Education

Smaller classes, thousands more teachers

The promise: hire the teachers needed to meet the state's 2022 class-size law — his February preliminary budget set aside $543 million for roughly 6,000 hires.

Scaled back

The May executive budget cut the commitment to $122 million for about 1,000 educators — a nearly 80% reduction — and Mamdani asked Albany to extend the class-size compliance deadline. Albany granted the delay June 1, pushing full compliance to 2029-30 — worth roughly $500 million to the city next year and, by one accounting, nearly $3 billion across the financial plan. Chalkbeat ↗ · Chalkbeat ↗

Needed City budget dollars, plus Albany's blessing to slow the mandate — the walk-back was City Hall's own choice.

Transit

Fare-free buses

The promise: make every city bus free — a cost Mamdani put at $600–700 million a year (the Metropolitan Transportation Authority chair says closer to $1 billion).

Blocked in Albany

Mamdani asked Albany in February and conceded by April that free buses would not happen in 2026. The final state budget included neither systemwide free buses nor a pilot. The city moved on adjacent fronts: stalled bus-lane projects were revived in February, a senior advisor for "fast and free buses" was named in late April and half-price Fair Fares expanded to about 340,000 more riders in the June budget. Streetsblog ↗ · New York Focus ↗

Needed Albany money and MTA cooperation — neither materialized.

Taxes

Tax the rich to pay for it all

The promise: a 2-percentage-point surtax on incomes over $1 million (roughly $3 billion a year) plus a higher corporate tax rate.

Blocked in Albany

The Legislature's one-house budgets endorsed corporate tax increases, but Hochul refused both taxes and the final state budget raised neither (it extended the existing top corporate rate rather than letting it lapse). Albany instead delivered a substitute package: a first-ever pied-à-terre tax on second homes over $5 million (projected at $500 million a year; the city comptroller estimates $340–380 million) and roughly $8 billion in state assistance over two years. New York Focus ↗ · The American Prospect ↗

Needed The State Legislature (partly on board) and the governor (opposed).

Health & family

Baby baskets for new parents

The promise: a free basket of newborn supplies — diapers, wipes, postpartum items — for every family, covering roughly 125,000 births a year at an estimated $19 million.

No visible action

Six months of news coverage and mayoral press releases show no implementation steps; the independent Mamdani Tracker, as of its last update in April, lists the promise as not started. Mamdani Tracker ↗

Needed Only city action — a health department program and a modest budget line.

Public safety

Disband the NYPD Strategic Response Group

The promise: dissolve the Police Department's protest-response unit, long criticized over its handling of demonstrations.

No visible action

By March, reporting found the administration "still discussing" how to disband the unit and Mamdani tempering his rhetoric; no disbandment had been announced as of July 1, and the unit remained funded in the executive budget. In April he also stepped back from a related pledge to eliminate the department's gang database, signaling openness to a reformed version. Gothamist ↗ · Gothamist ↗

Needed Mayoral and commissioner action alone.

Beyond the platform Major first-half moves that were not campaign promises.
June 30

A first budget deal, on time

The city is required by law to balance its budget; the choices within it are the story. The $125.8 billion deal passed the City Council 45–6, closing a projected $5.4 billion gap without major service cuts while expanding vouchers, Fair Fares and childcare and holding police headcount flat at about 35,000. Fiscal monitors note it leans on billions in one-time fixes — reserves, a pension-payment stretch and delayed costs — with a gap of more than $7 billion already projected for next year. amNewYork ↗ · The City Reporter ↗

May

An $8 billion state-aid haul

What Albany refused in taxes it partly replaced in aid: roughly $8 billion in state assistance over two fiscal years, announced jointly with the governor, alongside the new pied-à-terre tax. The American Prospect ↗ · NBC New York ↗

Jan–Feb

Homelessness policy, tested fast

Paused encampment sweeps on Jan. 5, then reinstated them Feb. 18 after a deadly cold snap — shifting the lead role from police to the Department of Homeless Services, with one-week notice periods. amNewYork ↗ · Fortune ↗

April 24

A deed-theft prevention office

Created the Mayor's Office of Deed Theft Prevention to combat home-title fraud, an issue concentrated in majority-Black and Hispanic homeowner neighborhoods. Mayor's office ↗ · amNewYork ↗

June 26

$15 million for gender-affirming care

Announced a $15 million investment in gender-affirming care access — a provider fund, a hotline and research, with an adult hormone-therapy pilot in Queens — as federal restrictions tightened. Mayor's office ↗ · Gothamist ↗

How we did this

The promise list reflects the twelve most prominent commitments of the 2025 Mamdani campaign, as documented in campaign materials and contemporaneous coverage. Status labels are ours: "delivered" means the specific promised outcome occurred; "in progress" means concrete, funded steps exist but the promise is unfinished; "scaled back" means the administration is pursuing a materially smaller version; "blocked in Albany" means the state refused required money or authority; "no visible action" means we found no implementation steps in six months of news coverage and mayoral press releases — absence of coverage, not proof of inaction. Every status claim links to at least one named source. Dollar figures are as reported by the linked outlets; where estimates conflict (bus costs, pied-à-terre revenue), we show the range.
